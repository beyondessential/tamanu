Summary

A health record for every patient and every health worker

Tamanu is an electronic medical record built for hospitals and clinics in low-resource, often low-connectivity settings across the Pacific and beyond. Inclusion is part of that brief: a system that only works for some patients, in some places, for some staff, does not do its job.

This document sets out three things:

  • what GEDSI means, and how Tamanu supports it in the way care is reached, recorded and protected
  • what accessibility means in software, and the international standard it is measured against
  • how Tamanu meets that standard today, and where the work continues

In short. Tamanu works offline and in the languages a deployment chooses, records the data needed to see inequity, protects the most sensitive health information, and builds on established accessibility practice: semantic controls, assistive technology support, adjustable text size and high contrast on patient-facing screens.

GEDSI and accessibility

Section 1

What GEDSI means

GEDSI stands for Gender Equality, Disability and Social Inclusion. It is a lens used across development and health programmes to ask who benefits from a service, who is left out, and why. It asks a programme to recognise that women, men and gender-diverse people, people with disability, and people marginalised by location, language, ethnicity, age or income meet different barriers, and to design so those barriers come down.

For a health information system, GEDSI has two sides. The system has to be usable by everyone who delivers and receives care. And the data it holds has to make inequity visible, so it can be acted on.

Section 2

How Tamanu supports GEDSI

Reaching people wherever they are

Remote and rural communities are among those most often excluded from health services. Tamanu is built so that distance and connectivity do not decide who gets a proper health record.

  • Works offline. Each facility runs its own Tamanu server, so care continues when the internet does not. Records synchronise with the central server whenever a connection returns.
  • Goes to the community. The Tamanu mobile app runs on Android devices at smaller facilities and on outreach visits, holding the records it needs on the device itself.
  • Built for low-resource settings. Tamanu runs on modest hardware and is designed for the realities of Pacific health systems.

Speaking the patient's and health worker's language

  • Every word in the interface can be translated. Each deployment chooses its languages and can adjust wording to suit local terms.
  • Health workers choose their language on the login screen, on both desktop and mobile.
  • Certificates and printouts, such as vaccine certificates and discharge summaries, use the deployment's translations.
  • Patient-facing tools, the self check-in kiosk and the patient portal, appear in the deployment's languages, and the kiosk lets each patient choose theirs.

Making inequity visible in the data

Inequity that is not measured is easy to overlook. Tamanu records the details needed to break health data down by who it concerns.

  • Sex is recorded for every patient as male, female or other.
  • Social and cultural details such as ethnicity, religion, educational attainment, occupation and a cultural or traditional name can be recorded, with each deployment choosing which apply.
  • Location, from village to catchment and facility, shows where services are and are not reaching.
  • Reports draw on these fields, so programmes can disaggregate results by sex, age, location and other characteristics.
  • Forms are configured by each deployment, so programmes can add the questions they need, such as a disability screening question set.

Protecting sensitive health information

Some care is only sought when patients trust that it stays private. Tamanu gives deployments the tools to protect it.

  • Sensitive networks keep confidential records within a defined group of facilities, so they never reach facilities outside it.
  • Sensitive medications and lab tests are shown only to staff with permission to see them.
  • Role-based permissions control what each staff member can see and do.

Including people with disability

  • Patients can check in on the self check-in kiosk with larger text and high contrast, and in their own language.
  • The patient portal works on the patient's own phone, with large touch-friendly controls and support for saved contact details.
  • Tamanu's interfaces are built to work with assistive technology, as set out in section 4.

GEDSI at a glance. Access regardless of location and connectivity. Choice of language. Data that can be broken down by sex and social characteristics. Privacy for sensitive care. Accessible patient-facing tools.

Section 3

Accessibility in software

Accessible software can be used by everyone, including people with disability, without needing help from someone else.

The World Health Organization estimates that around one in six people worldwide live with significant disability. Accessibility also helps far more people than that. A barrier can be:

  • permanent, such as low vision, deafness or limited use of the hands
  • temporary, such as a broken arm or an eye injury
  • situational, such as bright sunlight on a screen, a noisy ward, or gloved hands

People use assistive technologies to get around these barriers: screen readers that read the screen aloud, screen magnifiers, voice control, and keyboards or switches in place of a mouse or touch. Software is accessible when it works with these tools.

The international standard: WCAG

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), published by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), are the internationally recognised standard for accessible digital services. The current version, WCAG 2.2, is organised around four principles.

P

Perceivable

Information can be seen, heard or read in more than one way: text alternatives for images, enough contrast, text that can be enlarged.

O

Operable

Everything can be used with a keyboard or assistive device, controls are large enough to hit, and nothing moves or times out unexpectedly.

U

Understandable

Plain language, predictable layouts, and clear help when something goes wrong.

R

Robust

Built with standard, well-structured code so browsers and assistive technologies can interpret it reliably.

Each guideline has success criteria at three levels: A (essential), AA (the level most laws and procurement policies require) and AAA (enhanced). Level AA is the widely accepted benchmark for public and health services.

Section 4

How Tamanu meets accessibility standards

Tamanu follows WCAG 2.2 as its reference for accessible design and development. The table sets out how each principle shows up in the product.

PrincipleIn Tamanu
Perceivable A consistent colour system where each colour carries one meaning, with darker variants used wherever a colour carries text. Adjustable text size and a high contrast mode on the self check-in kiosk. Decorative icons are hidden from screen readers so they do not add noise.
Operable Every action is a real button or link, so it can be reached and used from the keyboard. Visible focus indicators show where the keyboard is. Large touch targets on the kiosk and patient portal, which grow with the chosen text size. Animation is switched off for people who ask their device to reduce motion.
Understandable Plain, consistent wording maintained through a single translation system, in the user's chosen language. Consistent layouts across screens. Form errors are flagged on the field they relate to and announced to assistive technology.
Robust Built on standard web technologies and a widely used component library. Controls carry accessible names, states and roles, such as expanded, selected or busy, so screen readers report them correctly.

Accessibility is part of how Tamanu is built

  • Development rules. Tamanu's coding standards require semantic buttons and links for every interactive element, and prefer standard accessibility attributes over custom equivalents. Code review checks for both.
  • Design system. A shared design system keeps colour, type and components consistent, so accessible patterns carry across every screen.
  • Patient-facing first. The screens patients use themselves, the kiosk and portal, carry the strongest accessibility settings, because their users are the widest group.

Working with partners

Tamanu is shaped by the ministries, hospitals and health workers who use it. We welcome feedback on any barrier a patient or staff member meets, and treat accessibility issues as defects to be fixed, not features to be scheduled.

Contact. To raise an accessibility or inclusion issue, or to discuss GEDSI requirements for a deployment, contact your Tamanu project manager or Beyond Essential Systems.
